## 1. Recap: Generative Models and Autoencoders

Last lecture introduced generative models, which model the joint distribution of data $P(X,Y)$ in order to generate new data $X$. It also introduced the autoencoder, an unsupervised model that learns to reduce the dimensionality of data $X$ into a more compact, latent space $z$, and then reconstruct these $X$ from the latent space.

### Two Paths to $P(Y|X)$

Two ways of predicting a label $Y$ from an input $X$, given observations $X, Y$:

- **Discriminative models:** Learn $P(Y\|X)$ directly.
- **Generative models:** Learn $P(X\|Y)$ and $P(Y)$, then calculate:

<d-math block>
P(X)=\int_Y P(X|Y)P(Y)\,dY,
\qquad P(Y|X)=\frac{P(X|Y)P(Y)}{P(X)}
</d-math>

### Deep Generative Models

Deep generative models use neural networks to represent complex distributions. Multiple layer of hidden variables hope to capture the hidden structure $z$ that explains high-dimensional $X$ values.

Because we do not observe $z$, two important computations become difficult:

- **Marginal likelihood:** <d-math>p_\theta(x)=\int p_\theta(x,z)\,dz</d-math>
- **Posterior inference:** <d-math>p_\theta(z\mid x) \propto p_\theta(x\mid z)p(z)</d-math>

Each type of deep generative model makes a tradeoff.

### Autoencoders

An autoencoder consists of an **encoder**, which maps an input $X$ to a latent space $z$, and a **decoder**, which reconstructs the input from that latent space.

**image 1 here**

<d-math block>
\mathcal{L} (x,x')=\|x-x'\|_2^2=\sum_i(x_i-{x'}_i)^2
</d-math>

This bottleneck enables the autoencoder to capture a useful structure. However, without knowing the distribution of this latent space $z$, we cannot conveniently use the autoencoder to generate new $X$: a randomly chosen $z$ may fall in a region the decoder has never learned to use.

A VAE addresses this problem by introducing a prior $p(z)$, allowing us to sample $z$ values.

## 2. Variational Autoencoders (VAEs)

The goal is to shape the latent space $z$ into a chosen prior distribution $p(z)$. Once the model is trained, this allows us to sample from this distribution and feed it through the decoder to generate new $x'$. This introduces a new problem: the encoder needs to learn to map inputs $x$ to our chosen latent space. To achieve this, rather than mapping each $x$ to a single point in $z$, the encoder will map inputs to conditional distributions $q(z\|x)$ that align with our prior p(z).

### The Encoder: Variational Inference

We want to approximate the true posterior $p(z|x)$. Variational inference is a method that will allow the encoder to learn a proxy for this posterior.

The main idea is to choose a family of distributions parameterized by **variational parameters** $v$,

<d-math block>
q(z|v)
</d-math>

then find $v$ that minimizes the discrepancy between q and the true posterior. Restricting the distributional family to known distributions makes this feasible.

**image 2 here**

In a VAE, an encoder network, with weights $\phi$, learns the parameters of $q_{\phi}(z|x)$. Our measure of how close q is to the posterior of interest is the KL divergence:

<d-math block>
D_{\text{KL}}(q_{\phi}(z|x)\|p_{\theta}(z|x))
</d-math>

### The Decoder

The ideal generation process:

1. $z$ is sampled from the true prior $p_{\theta^*}(z)$
2. $x$ is sampled from the true conditional $p_{\theta^*}(x|z^{(i)})$

To represent this model, we will choose the prior $p(z)$ to be a simple distribution, such as a Gaussian. Since the conditional $p(x|z)$ is more complex, we will represent it with a neural network.

To train the model, we'd ideally maximize the likelihood of training data:

<d-math block>
p_{\theta}(x) = \int p_{\theta}(z)p_{\theta}(x|z)\,dz
</d-math>

### Bringing it Together

The decoder network has parameters <d-math>\theta</d-math>, describing a distribution over reconstructed observations. It predicts a mean and diagonal covariance for <d-math>x\mid z</d-math>.

The encoder network has parameters <d-math>\phi</d-math>, describes a distribution over the latent space. It predicts a mean and diagonal covariance for <d-math>z\mid x</d-math>.

**image 3 here**

### Deriving the Loss Function

Start with the log-likelihood of the observation $x$ under the model parameterized by $\theta$. We take the expectation over the posterior distribution produced by the encoder network (they are equivalent since $p_\theta(x^{(i)})$ does not depend on $z$).

<d-math block>
\log p_\theta(x^{(i)})
=\mathbb{E}_{z \sim q_\phi(z|x^{(i)})}\left[
\log p_\theta(x^{(i)})
\right]
</d-math>

The next steps are an application of Bayes' Rule, arithmetic, and the definition of KL divergence:

<d-math block>
=\mathbb{E}_{z}\left[
\log \frac{p_\theta(x^{(i)}|z)p_\theta(z)}{p_\theta(z|x^{(i)})}
\right] \\
=\mathbb{E}_{z}\left[
\log \frac{p_\theta(x^{(i)}|z)p_\theta(z)}{p_\theta(z|x^{(i)})} \frac{q_\phi(z|x^{(i)})}{q_\phi(z|x^{(i)})}
\right] \\
= \mathbb{E}_{z}\left[ \log p_\theta(x^{(i)}|z) \right] - \mathbb{E}_{z}\left[ \log \frac{q_\phi(z|x^{(i)})}{p_\theta(z)} \right] + \mathbb{E}_{z}\left[ \log \frac{q_\phi(z|x^{(i)})}{p_\theta(z|x^{(i)})} \right] \\
=\mathbb{E}_{z}\left[\log p_\theta(x^{(i)}|z)\right]
- D_{\text{KL}}(q_\phi(z|x^{(i)})\|p_\theta(z))
+ D_{\text{KL}}(q_\phi(z|x^{(i)})\|p_\theta(z|x^{(i)}))
</d-math>

The last term is nonnegative, so the first two terms form an **evidence lower bound (ELBO)**:

<d-math block>
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ELBO}}(x;\theta,\phi)
=\mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z\mid x)}[\log p_\theta(x\mid z)]
-D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_\phi(z\mid x)\|p(z))
\leq\log p_\theta(x)
</d-math>

The gap between the bound and the log likelihood is the KL divergence to the true posterior. The bound becomes tight when the approximate posterior equals the true posterior.

Training maximizes the ELBO, or equivalently minimizes its negative:

<d-math block>
L_{\mathrm{VAE}}(x)
=-\mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z\mid x)}[\log p_\theta(x\mid z)]
+D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_\phi(z\mid x)\|p(z))
</d-math>

The two terms serve different purposes:

- **Reconstruction term:** Encourages the decoder to assign high probability to the original input given its inferred latent code.
- **Prior regularization term:** Penalizes departures of the encoder distribution from the prior, helping make codes sampled from the prior useful for generation.

The reconstruction expectation can be estimated by sampling. With a diagonal Gaussian encoder and standard normal prior, the regularization term has a closed form:

<d-math block>
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_\phi(z\mid x)\|\mathcal{N}(0,I))
=\frac{1}{2}\sum_j\left(\mu_j^2+\sigma_j^2-1-\log\sigma_j^2\right)
</d-math>

Here, <d-math>j</d-math> indexes latent dimensions. For a Gaussian decoder with fixed variance, the negative log likelihood is proportional to squared reconstruction error, up to constants.

### Putting it Together

For each mini-batch, the VAE performs the following steps:

1. Pass each input through the encoder to obtain its latent mean and variance.
2. Sample a latent code from the approximate posterior.
3. Pass that code through the decoder to obtain the reconstruction distribution.
4. Compute the negative log likelihood of the original input and add the KL penalty.
5. Average the loss over the mini-batch and update both networks through backpropagation.

To differentiate through latent sampling, a Gaussian VAE uses the **reparameterization trick**:

<d-math block>
\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I),\qquad
z=\mu_\phi(x)+\sigma_\phi(x)\odot\epsilon
</d-math>

The random noise is independent of the encoder parameters, while the transformation producing <d-math>z</d-math> is differentiable. The symbol <d-math>\odot</d-math> denotes element-wise multiplication.

The reconstruction loss evaluates the observed input using the decoder's distribution; an additional sampled output image is not needed to compute that likelihood.

### Generation

After training, generate new examples using only the decoder:

<d-math block>
z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I),\qquad \hat{x}\sim p_\theta(x\mid z)
</d-math>

During reconstruction, the latent code comes from <d-math>q_\phi(z\mid x)</d-math>. During generation, it comes directly from the prior, so no input example or encoder is required. The decoder mean can also be displayed as a representative output.

The slides show a grid of generated digits and examples of generated faces. Basic VAEs often produce blurred images, reflecting both their reconstruction likelihood and the pressure to represent a broad range of data. The bonus section returns to this mode-covering behavior.

## Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs)

GANs learn to generate data through an adversarial game between a generator and a discriminator. Introduced by Goodfellow and colleagues in 2014, they are especially associated with image synthesis, including realistic synthetic faces, but the framework applies to other data domains as well.

The goal is to learn a distribution that produces new examples resembling the training data. Like the VAE decoder, a typical GAN generator produces an entire image in one forward pass, rather than generating one component at a time as an autoregressive model does.

The slides also include DALL-E 2 and Imagen as examples of text-based image generation. Those examples illustrate diffusion-based generation, a different approach from GANs.

### Discriminator and Generator

The two networks have complementary roles:

- **Generator <d-math>G_\theta(z)</d-math>:** Transforms a random latent vector <d-math>z\sim p(z)</d-math> into a synthetic example. Its aim is to make the discriminator classify that example as real.
- **Discriminator <d-math>D_\phi(x)</d-math>:** Outputs the probability that an input is real rather than generated. Its aim is to distinguish training examples from generator outputs.

The generator defines an **implicit distribution** <d-math>p_g(x)</d-math>: we can draw samples by running the network, even though it need not provide a tractable expression for their density.

The discriminator supplies a learned training signal for the generator. A standard GAN does not require an encoder or a paired target image for each random code.

The slides' DCGAN example uses a convolutional generator: a 100-dimensional latent vector is projected into a small spatial feature map, then successive transposed convolutions increase its spatial resolution to a <d-math>64\times64</d-math> RGB image.

### Training

The original GAN objective is a minimax game:

<d-math block>
\min_\theta\max_\phi V(D_\phi,G_\theta)
=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_{\mathrm{data}}}[\log D_\phi(x)]
+\mathbb{E}_{z\sim p(z)}[\log(1-D_\phi(G_\theta(z)))]
</d-math>

The discriminator maximizes this expression, assigning high probabilities to real examples and low probabilities to generated ones. The generator minimizes it, encouraging the discriminator to assign high probabilities to its outputs.

Training alternates between two updates:

1. **Update the discriminator:** Sample real examples and noise vectors, generate fake examples, and maximize the real/fake classification objective while holding the generator fixed.
2. **Update the generator:** Sample noise vectors and minimize the generator objective while holding the discriminator's parameters fixed. Gradients pass through the discriminator into the generator.

There may be several discriminator steps per generator step. Expectations are approximated using mini-batches.

If both updates use gradient descent, their original losses are

<d-math block>
L_D=-\frac{1}{B}\sum_{i=1}^{B}
\left[\log D_\phi(x_i)+\log(1-D_\phi(G_\theta(z_i)))\right]
</d-math>

<d-math block>
L_G^{\mathrm{minimax}}=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{i=1}^{B}
\log(1-D_\phi(G_\theta(z_i)))
</d-math>

where <d-math>B</d-math> is the mini-batch size. The sign difference reflects the discriminator maximizing the game objective while the generator minimizes it.

At an ideal equilibrium, the generator distribution matches the data distribution and the optimal discriminator outputs <d-math>1/2</d-math>. Neither player can improve its objective by changing its own strategy alone. In practice, discriminator outputs near <d-math>1/2</d-math> do not by themselves prove successful learning; an undertrained discriminator can also be uncertain.

### Training Problems

GAN training can be unstable because each network changes the learning problem faced by the other.

- **Oscillation:** Generator and discriminator performance can fluctuate rather than converge smoothly.
- **Mode collapse:** The generator may produce only a few kinds of examples, even when the training data contain many distinct kinds.
- **An overly strong discriminator:** It can confidently reject generated examples, leaving the original generator objective with a weak gradient.
- **An overly weak discriminator:** It can be fooled by unrealistic outputs and provide an unhelpful learning signal.
- **Hyperparameter sensitivity:** Learning rates and the balance of network updates affect training behavior.

The slides address weak generator gradients with the **non-saturating generator loss**:

<d-math block>
L_G^{\mathrm{non\text{-}saturating}}
=-\mathbb{E}_{z\sim p(z)}[\log D_\phi(G_\theta(z))]
</d-math>

Minimizing this loss is equivalent to maximizing the log probability that generated examples are classified as real. It provides a stronger learning signal when the discriminator confidently rejects fake examples. The discriminator objective stays the same.

This changes the generator's training objective while retaining the goal of making realistic samples. It does not, by itself, eliminate mode collapse or guarantee convergence.

## A Unified View (Bonus)

The bonus slides recast VAEs and GANs in a common probabilistic framework. Both couple a process that produces observations with a process that infers something about them, but their objectives and network roles differ.

### GANs: Rewritten

Introduce a binary source label <d-math>y</d-math>, where <d-math>y=0</d-math> means generated and <d-math>y=1</d-math> means real:

<d-math block>
p_\theta(x\mid y)=
\begin{cases}
p_{g_\theta}(x), & y=0 \\
p_{\mathrm{data}}(x), & y=1
\end{cases}
</d-math>

The generated branch is sampled implicitly using <d-math>x=G_\theta(z)</d-math>. The discriminator represents a conditional distribution over source labels:

<d-math block>
q_\phi(y=1\mid x)=D_\phi(x),\qquad
q_\phi(y=0\mid x)=1-D_\phi(x)
</d-math>

With equally weighted real and generated examples, discriminator training maximizes the conditional log likelihood of the correct source label. Generator training using the non-saturating objective maximizes the likelihood of the flipped label on generated examples.

The slides express this reversal as

<d-math block>
q_\phi^r(y\mid x)=q_\phi(1-y\mid x)
</d-math>

This exposes the relationship between generating an observation from a source and inferring its source from the observation. Here, <d-math>y</d-math> identifies real versus generated data; it is not a semantic class label.

### VAEs vs GANs

A VAE uses <d-math>q_\phi(z\mid x)</d-math> to infer latent codes and <d-math>p_\theta(x\mid z)</d-math> to reconstruct observations. Both networks optimize the same ELBO, including an explicit regularization term involving the latent prior.

A GAN uses <d-math>G_\theta</d-math> to generate observations and <d-math>q_\phi(y\mid x)</d-math> to classify their source. The two networks pursue competing objectives rather than maximizing a shared reconstruction bound.

The symmetry emphasized by the slides is a reversal of what is predicted:

- **VAE reconstruction:** Predict the observation <d-math>x</d-math> given an inferred latent code <d-math>z</d-math>.
- **GAN discrimination:** Predict the source label <d-math>y</d-math> given an observed or generated example <d-math>x</d-math>.

The bonus framework also includes an InfoGAN extension with a latent-code inference model. That extra inference network is not required by a standard GAN.

### Mode Covering vs Mode Collapse

A **mode** is a region of high probability, such as one cluster in a multimodal dataset. A model must balance producing realistic examples with representing the diversity of the data.

The bonus slides relate this balance to the asymmetry of KL divergence. In their unified notation, the VAE-like and GAN-like objectives have opposite divergence directions, schematically <d-math>D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q\|P_\theta)</d-math> and <d-math>D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_\theta\|Q)</d-math>.

The intuition can be understood using a data distribution <d-math>p</d-math> and model distribution <d-math>r</d-math>:

- **Mode covering:** Minimizing <d-math>D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|r)</d-math> strongly penalizes assigning little model probability where data occur. A limited model may spread probability across several modes, including low-density regions between them.
- **Mode seeking:** Minimizing <d-math>D_{\mathrm{KL}}(r\|p)</d-math> strongly penalizes placing model probability where the data density is small. A limited model may concentrate on one mode and miss others.

For example, if the data contain two separated clusters but the model can represent only one Gaussian, covering both clusters can produce a broad fit, while seeking one mode can produce a narrower fit that ignores the other cluster.

This gives intuition for the slides' comparison: basic VAEs often cover diverse possibilities but generate blurry images, whereas GANs can produce sharp images while missing parts of the data distribution. GAN mode collapse is the more severe failure in which many latent codes produce similar outputs.

These are tendencies, not guarantees. The bonus comparison does not mean that the original GAN minimax objective directly minimizes reverse KL between data and generated samples. Likewise, the KL term in the VAE ELBO acts on latent distributions; decoder assumptions and model capacity also affect blur.

### What to Remember

- **VAEs:** Train an encoder and decoder jointly by maximizing an ELBO, balancing reconstruction likelihood with latent prior regularization.
- **VAE generation:** Sample a latent code from the prior and run the decoder; the encoder is needed for inference, not for generating a new example.
- **GANs:** Train a generator to fool a discriminator that learns to distinguish real from generated examples.
- **GAN optimization:** Alternate network updates; the non-saturating generator loss helps when the original generator gradient is weak.
- **Main tradeoff:** Sample quality and distribution coverage are different goals. Sharp samples alone do not establish that a model represents all modes of the training data.
