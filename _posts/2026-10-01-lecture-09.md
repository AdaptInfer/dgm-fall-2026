## Section 1: Why We Need Regularization

### Where we left off

Last lecture we saw that weight sharing and sparse connectivity tame the parameter explosion of fully-connected layers, and that backprop through a convolution is itself a convolution (with the kernel flipped). But the punchline was: **CNNs can still overfit**, even though they have far fewer parameters than an equivalent fully-connected layer. So today's question is: *what should we do about overfitting?*

### Regularization is one tool among many

Regularization is one strategy to improve generalization, and it is just one of many ways to do it. The boundary between regularization and other engineering techniques is not always clear. A rough way to think about the whole pipeline is:

1. Dataset
2. Architecture setup
3. Normalization
4. Training loop
5. **Regularization**

The general strategies against overfitting are:

- **Collect more data**, especially high-quality data. This is the best option and always recommended (semi-supervised learning, transfer learning and self-supervised learning are ways to get more out of the data we have).
- **Data augmentation** is helpful.
- **Reducing model capacity** can help.

Note that training is basically maximum likelihood, so the model is only ever as good as the data it is fit on. That is why high-quality data comes first. Which of the other tools is best is "not entirely settled", and it depends on the problem.

### The bias-variance trade-off

The expected test error decomposes into three parts:

<d-math block>
\mathbb{E}[\text{test error}] = \text{bias}^2 + \text{variance} + \text{irreducible noise}.
</d-math>

- **Bias** is the error from a model that is too simple to capture the true pattern.
- **Variance** is the error from a model that is too sensitive to the specific training sample it happened to see.

Increasing capacity (more layers or units) typically lowers bias but raises variance. This gives us a useful way to look at every regularizer in this lecture: *each technique trades a little bias for a lot less variance.*<d-footnote>This is also how Goodfellow et al. put it: an effective regularizer is one that makes a profitable trade, reducing variance a lot without increasing bias too much.<d-cite key="goodfellow2016deep"></d-cite></d-footnote>

A concrete example from the lecture:

| Model | Train acc. | Test acc. | Diagnosis |
| ----- | ---------- | --------- | --------- |
| A: small MLP | 60% | 58% | Both low, so high bias (underfitting) |
| B: large MLP | 99% | 71% | Huge gap, so high variance (overfitting) |

Model B is actually the better model, even though it overfits. A large model that we then regularize usually beats starting with a small one.

**Rule of thumb:** if the training accuracy is low, look at bias. If the train/test gap is big, look at variance.

This is why the advice was: *first overfit a model, then regularize it*, instead of starting with a smaller model and hoping it is the right size.

## Section 2: More Data and Data Augmentation

More data is the cleanest way to reduce variance. Data is valuable, but often we cannot afford more of it. **Data augmentation** is the cheap alternative.

The idea: if we know the label should not depend on some transformation <d-math>h</d-math>, then for every training pair <d-math>(x_i, y_i)</d-math> we can generate a new pair <d-math>(h(x_i), y_i)</d-math>. This increases the diversity of the training data.

The catch is that **we must already know something our outcome does not depend on.** For image classification, the label should not change under, e.g., rotation, zooming or a sepia filter. But a transformation that changes the label is harmful. For digit recognition, flipping a "6" by 180° turns it into a "9"<d-cite key="goodfellow2016deep"></d-cite>.

In PyTorch this is done with TorchVision. The lecture example uses MNIST: the randomly augmented digits are slightly cropped and rotated versions of the originals, but still show the same digit, so the label stays the same. The key detail is that **random augmentation is applied only to the training data**. The test transform is deterministic (resize, then crop the center), so that the evaluation is reproducible:

<d-code block language="python">
import PIL
import torchvision
from torchvision import datasets

# Note transforms.ToTensor() scales input images to 0-1 range

training_transforms = torchvision.transforms.Compose([
    torchvision.transforms.Resize(size=(32, 32)),
    torchvision.transforms.RandomCrop(size=(28, 28)),
    torchvision.transforms.RandomRotation(degrees=30, interpolation=PIL.Image.BILINEAR),
    torchvision.transforms.ToTensor(),
    torchvision.transforms.Normalize(mean=(0.5,), std=(0.5,)),
    # normalize does (x_i - mean) / std
    # if images are [0, 1], they will be [-1, 1] afterwards
])

test_transforms = torchvision.transforms.Compose([
    torchvision.transforms.ToTensor(),
    torchvision.transforms.Resize(size=(32, 32)),
    torchvision.transforms.CenterCrop(size=(28, 28)),
    torchvision.transforms.Normalize(mean=(0.5,), std=(0.5,)),
])

train_dataset = datasets.MNIST(root='data',
                               train=True,
                               transform=training_transforms,
                               download=True)

test_dataset = datasets.MNIST(root='data',
                              train=False,
                              transform=test_transforms)
</d-code>

The two pipelines differ in exactly the random steps (`RandomCrop`, `RandomRotation` in training and `CenterCrop` at test time), while the final normalization to <d-math>[-1, 1]</d-math> is the same for both.<d-footnote>Code from the Lecture 9 slides, which come from the linked notebook <code>L10/code/data-augmentation.ipynb</code> in the repository rasbt/stat453-deep-learning-ss21.</d-footnote>

## Section 3: Reducing Capacity and Early Stopping

### The simplest model that fits should generalize best

Several knobs shrink the effective capacity of a network:

- **Smaller architecture**: fewer hidden layers and units.
- **Smaller weights**: early stopping, L2 norm penalty.
- **Sparse weights or activations**: ReLU with an L1 penalty prunes units.
- **Noise**: dropout.

For recent LLMs and foundation models there is another option: take a large pretrained model and do efficient **fine-tuning**. We freeze most of the weights and only train (fine-tune) the remaining small number of parameters.

### Three data splits

Early stopping needs us to be careful with the data splits. As always we split into three parts:

- **Training set**: used to update the weights.
- **Validation set**: used for tuning (hyperparameters, when to stop). It lives *inside* the training loop.
- **Test set**: used *once* at the end, outside the loop. In the end we care about the test accuracy, so we must not touch it before.

Some fields swap the names of test and validation (medicine, for example), so watch the terminology when reading papers.

### Early stopping

The idea is simple: watch the gap between training and validation performance during training, and stop at the "right point".

In practice we keep a copy of the best weights seen so far and stop once the validation performance has not improved for a number of epochs, called the *patience*. The code below monitors the validation **loss**, where lower is better. The figure above plots **accuracy**, where higher is better, so if you monitor accuracy instead, just flip the comparison (`val_acc > best_val`) and start from `-inf`:

<d-code block language="python">
best_val, best_state, bad_epochs, patience = float("inf"), None, 0, 5

for epoch in range(max_epochs):
    train_one_epoch(model, train_loader)
    val_loss = evaluate(model, val_loader)

    if val_loss < best_val:
        best_val, bad_epochs = val_loss, 0
        best_state = {k: v.clone() for k, v in model.state_dict().items()}
    else:
        bad_epochs += 1
        if bad_epochs >= patience:
            break

model.load_state_dict(best_state)   # go back to the best epoch
</d-code>

This makes early stopping an **implicit regularizer**, and not just a compute-saving trick. Why does it generalize better? Deep networks are initialized with small random weights, where many weights cancel out, so the network starts out behaving almost like a *linear* model. Training then makes it gradually more complex. Stopping early means it never gets to the very complex solutions that overfit.

There is also a precise version of this for linear models. With a quadratic loss, gradient descent with learning rate <d-math>\epsilon</d-math> run for <d-math>\tau</d-math> steps behaves like L2 regularization with <d-math>\alpha \approx 1/(\epsilon\tau)</d-math>. More training steps correspond to weaker regularization<d-cite key="goodfellow2016deep"></d-cite>.

## Section 4: Norm Penalties (L1 and L2)

### Adding a penalty to the loss

We add a penalty on the size of the weights to the loss:

<d-math block>
\tilde{J}(w) = J(w) + \lambda\, \Omega(w),
</d-math>

where <d-math>J</d-math> is the usual data loss and <d-math>\lambda \ge 0</d-math> controls how strongly we regularize.

- **L2** penalizes <d-math>\Omega(w)=\sum_i w_i^2</d-math>. This is the same idea as **ridge regression**, and it encourages *small values*.
- **L1** penalizes <d-math>\Omega(w)=\sum_i \lvert w_i \rvert</d-math>. This is the same idea as **LASSO**, and it encourages *zeros*, i.e. sparse solutions.

In neural networks we typically only penalize the weights and leave the biases alone<d-cite key="goodfellow2016deep"></d-cite>.

### Regularization is MAP estimation

In Lecture 3 we saw that MAP estimation with a Gaussian prior gives L2 and with a Laplace prior gives L1. Here is the derivation again. MAP maximizes the posterior <d-math>P(w \mid D)</d-math>, which by Bayes' rule is proportional to <d-math>P(D \mid w)\,P(w)</d-math>. Taking the negative log (like we did in Lecture 6 to go from a likelihood to a loss):

<d-math block>
-\log P(w \mid D) = \underbrace{-\log P(D \mid w)}_{\text{data loss}} \;\underbrace{-\log P(w)}_{\text{penalty from the prior}} + \text{const}.
</d-math>

So the penalty is nothing but the negative log of the prior:

| Prior <d-math>P(w)</d-math> | Penalty | Name |
| ------------ | ------- | ---- |
| Gaussian | <d-math>\sum_i w_i^2</d-math> | L2 / ridge |
| Laplace | <d-math>\sum_i \lvert w_i \rvert</d-math> | L1 / LASSO |

### Geometric picture

Penalizing the norm is the same as constraining the weights to lie in a region around the origin: a **circle** (ball) for L2 and a **diamond** (square rotated by 45°) for L1. The solution is the point where the contours of the data loss first touch the region.

The corners of the diamond lie on the axes, and that is why L1 produces sparse solutions while L2 only shrinks.

### Weight decay

For multilayer networks we simply sum the penalty over all layers. In the notation of the lecture, with <d-math>n</d-math> training examples, the L2-regularized cost is

<d-math block>
\text{Cost} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \mathcal{L}\big(y^{[i]}, \hat{y}^{[i]}\big) + \frac{\lambda}{n}\sum_{l=1}^{L} \big\lVert \mathbf{W}^{(l)} \big\rVert_F^2,
\qquad
\big\lVert \mathbf{W}^{(l)} \big\rVert_F^2 = \sum_i \sum_j \big(w_{i,j}^{(l)}\big)^2,
</d-math>

where <d-math>\lVert\cdot\rVert_F</d-math> is the (squared) Frobenius norm of the weight matrix of layer <d-math>l</d-math>. The gradient of the penalty with respect to a single weight is <d-math>\frac{2\lambda}{n} w_{i,j}</d-math>, so the gradient descent update with learning rate <d-math>\eta</d-math> changes from the regular update to the one with L2 regularization:

<d-math block>
w_{i,j} := w_{i,j} - \eta\,\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial w_{i,j}}
\qquad\longrightarrow\qquad
w_{i,j} := w_{i,j} - \eta\left(\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial w_{i,j}} + \frac{2\lambda}{n}\, w_{i,j}\right).
</d-math>

Rewriting the right-hand side gives <d-math>w_{i,j} := \big(1 - \tfrac{2\eta\lambda}{n}\big) w_{i,j} - \eta\,\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial w_{i,j}}</d-math>. Each step first multiplicatively shrinks the weight and then takes the usual step. This is why L2 regularization is also called **weight decay**<d-cite key="goodfellow2016deep"></d-cite>. For L1 the shrinkage is a constant amount in the direction of <d-math>\mathrm{sign}(w)</d-math> per step (independent of the weight size), which is what pushes small weights all the way to zero.

### L2 regularization in PyTorch

In PyTorch you can add the penalty to the cost manually, or let the optimizer do it with `weight_decay`:

<d-code block language="python">
# manually: only penalize the weights, not the biases
L2 = 0.
for name, p in model.named_parameters():
    if 'weight' in name:
        L2 = L2 + (p**2).sum()

cost = cost + 2./targets.size(0) * LAMBDA * L2

optimizer.zero_grad()
cost.backward()

# automatically: the optimizer adds the decay term to the gradient
optimizer = torch.optim.SGD(model.parameters(), lr=0.1, weight_decay=LAMBDA)
</d-code>

<d-footnote>The constant in front of the penalty only rescales the regularization strength λ, so conventions like λ/n or 2λ/n differ only in the choice of λ. With <code>weight_decay=LAMBDA</code>, SGD adds <code>LAMBDA * w</code> to the gradient, so <code>LAMBDA</code> plays the role of the factor in front of <code>w</code> in the update above. For adaptive optimizers like Adam this is not exactly the same as an L2 penalty, which is why <code>AdamW</code> exists.</d-footnote>

## Section 5: Dropout

Dropout is a deep-learning-specific method. When propagating forward during training, we randomly **drop some neurons by setting their activations to 0**.

### Bernoulli masks

With drop probability <d-math>p</d-math>, every unit gets an independent Bernoulli mask <d-math>m_i</d-math>:

<d-math block>
m_i = \begin{cases} 0 & \text{with probability } p \\ 1 & \text{with probability } 1-p \end{cases}
\qquad
\tilde{a}_i = m_i \, a_i .
</d-math>

In practice we sample <d-math>v \sim \mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)</d-math> for each unit and drop it if <d-math>v  \lt  p</d-math>. A new mask is drawn for every minibatch.

<d-code block language="python">
import torch.nn as nn

model = nn.Sequential(
    nn.Linear(784, 256),
    nn.ReLU(),
    nn.Dropout(drop_proba),   # drop_proba in [0, 1]
    nn.Linear(256, 10),
)
</d-code>

### Why does it work?

1. **No single connection can be relied on.** The network cannot depend on particular connections too heavily, so it has to use more of them. The weights end up more spread out, which may lead to smaller weights, similar to the L2 norm. (As a side note from the slides: you can use different dropout probabilities in different layers, and assigning them proportional to the number of units in a layer is not a bad idea.)
2. **Implicit ensemble.** Every minibatch trains a different sub-network. With <d-math>h</d-math> hidden units there are <d-math>M = 2^h</d-math> possible masks, so over the minibatch iterations we sample from an exponentially large family of models. These models **share weights**, which is itself a form of regularization. At inference time we could average over all of these models, but that is extremely expensive. This is where the trick below comes in.<d-footnote>Goodfellow et al. describe dropout as making bagging practical for exponentially many large networks.<d-cite key="goodfellow2016deep"></d-cite></d-footnote>
3. **It suppresses high-order interactions.** This is the view from the lecturer's own paper on dropout and interaction effects<d-cite key="lengerich2022dropout"></d-cite>. An *interaction of order <d-math>k</d-math>* is an effect that needs <d-math>k</d-math> variables at the same time (e.g. "feature A and feature B together"). Two things happen as <d-math>k</d-math> grows:
   - With <d-math>N</d-math> input variables there are <d-math>\binom{N}{k}</d-math> possible order-<d-math>k</d-math> interactions, which grows roughly like <d-math>N^k</d-math> for the first few orders. So there are many more high-order combinations that could fit noise in the training data.
   - An order-<d-math>k</d-math> interaction only survives a dropout step if all <d-math>k</d-math> of its variables survive. With drop rate <d-math>r</d-math> this has probability <d-math>(1-r)^k</d-math>, which decays exponentially in <d-math>k</d-math>.

   The slide then states that these exponential rates cancel out, and that this anti-interaction regularization happens at every layer.<d-footnote>The slide only has this one line about the cancellation. One way to read it is that the exponentially growing number of candidate interactions and the exponentially decaying survival probability work against each other, so it is not obvious in advance which one wins. The paper is what shows that the net effect is a suppression of higher orders.<d-cite key="lengerich2022dropout"></d-cite></d-footnote> The net effect is that dropout puts more pressure on higher-order effects than on low-order ones. In the experiment from the paper that is shown on the slide, the share of the normalized effect size that comes from first-order (additive) terms grows from roughly a quarter to roughly three quarters when the dropout rate goes from 0 to 0.75, while the higher orders shrink. A practical consequence: if you want to regularize away more complex interactions, increase the dropout rate.

### Inverted dropout and `train()` vs. `eval()`

If we drop units during training but use all units at test time, the expected input to the next layer changes. The fix is **inverted dropout**: scale the surviving activations by <d-math>1/(1-p)</d-math> *during training*:

<d-math block>
\tilde{a}_i = \frac{m_i}{1-p}\, a_i .
</d-math>

Then the expected activation is unchanged, and at inference time we do **nothing**. This is what most frameworks actually implement. It is helpful because a model is trained once but used many times at "test" time, so we want inference to be free of extra operations.

In PyTorch the behavior depends on the mode of the model:

- `model.train()` turns dropout **on**. Use this in the training loop.
- `model.eval()` turns dropout **off**. Use this when you just want to see how good the model is.

<d-code block language="python">
model.train()
for x, y in train_loader:
    ...

model.eval()
with torch.no_grad():
    val_acc = evaluate(model, val_loader)
</d-code>

Forgetting `model.eval()` is a classic bug: the validation numbers come out noisy and too pessimistic, because dropout is still active.

The slides compare training with and without dropout on MNIST with a multilayer perceptron. Without dropout, the training accuracy goes towards 100% while the validation accuracy plateaus at around 97 to 98%, so there is a visible gap. With 50% dropout, the training and validation curves lie almost on top of each other at roughly 97.5%, so the gap is gone. The minibatch loss is noisier and stays higher, because every step uses a randomly thinned network.

## Key Takeaways

- Overfitting is a **variance** problem. Every regularizer trades a bit of bias for a lot less variance.
- Low train accuracy means look at bias. A big train/test gap means look at variance. In practice, first overfit a big model, then regularize.
- **More (and more diverse) data** is the best fix. Augmentation only helps if we know a transformation the label does not depend on, and it is applied only to the training data.
- **Early stopping** uses the validation set to pick the stopping point and acts as an implicit regularizer. The test set is touched only once.
- **L2** (ridge, Gaussian prior) encourages small weights. **L1** (LASSO, Laplace prior) encourages zeros. Both are just the negative log of a prior added to the data loss.
- **Dropout** randomly zeroes units during training, which prevents co-adaptation and approximates averaging over an exponential ensemble of weight-sharing networks. Use `model.train()` and `model.eval()` correctly.
