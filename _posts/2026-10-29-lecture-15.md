## Introduction to Diffusion Models

The main idea behind diffusion models is to take an image, gradually add noise until the original image is no longer recognizable, and then train a model to reverse that process. Once the model learns how to remove noise step by step, it can start with random noise and generate a completely new image.

There are two separate processes involved. The forward process adds noise to an image, while the reverse process learns how to remove it. The forward process is predetermined, meaning we already know how much noise is being added at each step. The reverse process is what the model actually needs to learn.

<d-math block>
x_0 \rightarrow x_1 \rightarrow \cdots \rightarrow x_T
</d-math>

<d-math block>
x_T \rightarrow x_{T-1} \rightarrow \cdots \rightarrow x_0
</d-math>

Here, <d-math>x_0</d-math> represents the original image, <d-math>x_t</d-math> represents the image after some amount of noise has been added, and <d-math>x_T</d-math> represents the final noisy image.

## The Forward Diffusion Process

The forward process gradually adds Gaussian noise to an image over a series of timesteps. Rather than adding all the noise at once, each step adds a small amount while reducing the contribution of the previous image.

The forward transition is defined as

<d-math block>
q(x_t \mid x_{t-1}) =
\mathcal{N}\left(x_t;\sqrt{1-\beta_t}x_{t-1},\beta_t I\right)
</d-math>

The parameter <d-math>\beta_t</d-math> controls the amount of noise added at timestep <d-math>t</d-math>. A larger value of <d-math>\beta_t</d-math> means that more noise is introduced during that step.

The term <d-math>\sqrt{1-\beta_t}</d-math> scales down the previous image before the new noise is added. This matters because the process is not simply adding more and more noise on top of an unchanged image. The original information gradually becomes weaker as the noise increases.

Another important detail is that each step only depends on the previous noisy image. The process does not need to reference the original image every time noise is added. This is known as the Markov property.

The complete forward process can be written as

<d-math block>
q(x_{1:T}\mid x_0)
=
\prod_{t=1}^{T}q(x_t\mid x_{t-1})
</d-math>

Since the noise schedule is chosen beforehand, the forward process does not need to be learned. The main challenge is learning how to reverse it.

## The Reverse Diffusion Process

The reverse process attempts to recover a less noisy image from the current noisy image. Instead of removing all the noise in one step, the model makes a series of predictions that gradually produce a cleaner result.

The reverse transition is modeled as

<d-math block>
p_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)
=
\mathcal{N}\left(x_{t-1};\mu_\theta(x_t,t),\Sigma_\theta(x_t,t)\right)
</d-math>

Here, <d-math>\mu_\theta</d-math> represents the predicted mean of the previous state, while <d-math>\Sigma_\theta</d-math> represents the covariance. Both describe the distribution used to produce a less noisy sample.

The timestep <d-math>t</d-math> is also included as an input. This is important because the amount of noise changes throughout the process. A sample near <d-math>x_T</d-math> contains significantly more noise than one near <d-math>x_0</d-math>, so the model needs to know which stage of denoising it is working on.

The true reverse distribution <d-math>q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)</d-math> is generally unknown. Because of this, a neural network is used to approximate the reverse transitions through the learned distribution <d-math>p_\theta</d-math>.

The complete reverse process is

<d-math block>
p_\theta(x_{0:T})
=
p(x_T)\prod_{t=1}^{T}p_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)
</d-math>

### Generating a New Sample

Generating a new image begins by sampling <d-math>x_T</d-math> from a noise distribution. The reverse model then predicts <d-math>x_{T-1}</d-math>, followed by <d-math>x_{T-2}</d-math>, continuing until it reaches <d-math>x_0</d-math>.

Each step produces a slightly less noisy sample. By the final step, the result is a generated image.

An original clean image is not required during generation because the model has already learned how to perform the reverse transitions during training.

## Predicting Noise During Training

One way to train a diffusion model is to have it predict the noise that was added to a training example.

The noise-prediction objective can be written as

<d-math block>
\mathbb{E}_{t,x_0,\epsilon}
\left[
w(t)\left\|
\epsilon-\epsilon_\theta(x_t,t)
\right\|_2^2
\right]
</d-math>

In this equation, <d-math>\epsilon</d-math> is the actual noise used to corrupt the training image, while <d-math>\epsilon_\theta(x_t,t)</d-math> is the noise predicted by the model.

The difference between the actual noise and the predicted noise is squared to measure the prediction error. The goal during training is to minimize this difference so that the model becomes better at identifying noise at different timesteps.

The term <d-math>w(t)</d-math> allows different timesteps to contribute different amounts to the training objective.

This objective is related to optimizing a variational bound on the negative log-likelihood. With Gaussian noise schedules, the training objective can be expressed in terms of the difference between actual and predicted noise.

## Latent Diffusion Models

One limitation of diffusion models is the computational cost of repeatedly processing images in pixel space. Images can contain a large number of pixel values, and applying the denoising process across many timesteps can require significant computation.

Latent diffusion addresses this by performing the diffusion process in a compressed representation rather than directly on the original image.

An encoder first maps the image into a lower-dimensional latent representation. The diffusion process then operates in this latent space. Once denoising is complete, a decoder converts the latent representation back into an image.

<d-math block>
x \xrightarrow{\text{encoder}} z
</d-math>

<d-math block>
z_T \rightarrow z_{T-1} \rightarrow \cdots \rightarrow z_0
</d-math>

<d-math block>
z_0 \xrightarrow{\text{decoder}} \hat{x}
</d-math>

Here, <d-math>z</d-math> represents the compressed image information. Instead of repeatedly modifying every pixel, the model works with this smaller representation.

This reduces the amount of information that needs to be processed during diffusion while still allowing the decoder to reconstruct an image from the final latent representation.

## Text Conditioning and Stable Diffusion

Diffusion models can also be conditioned on additional information, such as text. This allows the generated image to be influenced by a text prompt rather than relying only on the learned image distribution.

In text-conditioned latent diffusion, the model uses both the current noisy latent representation and information from the text prompt when predicting the next denoising step.

A conditional reverse transition can be expressed as

<d-math block>
p_\theta(z_{t-1}\mid z_t,c)
</d-math>

Here, <d-math>c</d-math> represents the conditioning information from the text.

The conditioning information guides the reverse process toward images that correspond to the provided text. For example, a prompt describing a particular object or scene influences what the model attempts to generate as it removes noise.

Stable Diffusion combines latent diffusion with text conditioning to generate images based on written descriptions.

## Comparison with Other Generative Models

Variational autoencoders, generative adversarial networks, and diffusion models all generate new samples, but they use different approaches.

| Model | Generation Process | Main Characteristics |
|:--|:--|:--|
| VAE | Samples a latent representation and decodes it into an image | Tends to cover different modes of the data distribution, but generated samples can appear blurry |
| GAN | Transforms random noise into an image using a generator | Can generate realistic samples, but training can be unstable and mode collapse can occur |
| Diffusion | Starts with random noise and gradually denoises it | Uses a fixed forward noising process and a learned reverse process, with a tendency toward mode coverage |

VAEs rely on a latent distribution and a decoder, while GANs use adversarial training between a generator and discriminator.

Diffusion models instead learn a sequence of reverse transitions. Rather than producing the entire image in one step, the image is gradually constructed through repeated denoising.

## Conclusion

Diffusion models generate new data by learning to reverse a gradual noising process. The forward process is fixed and adds Gaussian noise over multiple timesteps, while the reverse process uses a learned model to progressively remove that noise.

The noise schedule determines how information is corrupted, and the timestep helps the model identify how much noise is present. Training can be performed by comparing the noise predicted by the model with the actual noise added to the data.

Latent diffusion makes the process more computationally efficient by operating in a compressed representation, while text conditioning allows the generation process to be guided by written prompts.

The main difference between diffusion models and other generative approaches is that samples are generated through a sequence of learned denoising steps rather than through a single decoding or generation operation.
