abstract: >
  Pretraining teaches a language model to predict the next token of internet text by maximum likelihood.
  But likelihood is not usefulness: a model trained this way copies its corpus, puts probability on incoherent text, and has no goal.
  The result is a model that is fluent but not reliably helpful or truthful.
  Post-training fixes this by optimizing a reward instead of likelihood.
  This lecture covers three sources of that reward: demonstrations (SFT), a reward model learned from human preferences (RLHF),
  and the preference pairs themselves (DPO), which turn out to define the same objective as RLHF without a reward model or an RL loop.

## 1. Why likelihood is not enough

Pretraining teaches a model which text is **likely**, but likely text is not always **correct**. Maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) has no notion of truth, no task goal, and no reward, and these gaps show up in what the model learns and in how it generates.

### 1.1 The model learns the corpus, not what is right

MLE is equivalent to minimizing the forward KL divergence from the training corpus to the model:

$$
\arg\max_\theta \mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_{\text{corpus}}}[\log p_\theta(x)] = \arg\min_\theta \mathrm{KL}(p_{\text{corpus}} \,\|\, p_\theta).
$$

The target here is the corpus, not the world. MLE never checks whether a sentence is true; it only learns how often sentences like it appear. Duplicates, spam, toxic text, and stale facts therefore get probability in proportion to their frequency, and a document repeated $k$ times counts $k$ times. A false claim that appears often becomes likely, and the model has no way to tell that it is wrong.
Because frequency is all MLE sees, data selection becomes part of the objective: filtering and deduplication change what the model learns. RefinedWeb, for example, kept only about 12% of Common Crawl <d-cite key="penedo2023refinedweb"></d-cite>.

### 1.2 The model spreads probability onto bad text

The forward KL is large wherever the corpus has mass but the model does not, so the model must cover every mode of the data. A model with limited capacity cannot cover them all tightly, and some of its probability lands between the modes, on incoherent text. This behavior is called **mode-covering**.

Mode-covering makes generation fail in two directions <d-cite key="holtzman2020curious"></d-cite>. Sampling from the full distribution eventually draws from the unreliable tail, and every later token builds on the mistake; prompted with a story about English-speaking unicorns, pure sampling invents "Bolivian Cavalleros" and drifts into nonsense. Always picking the most likely token, as greedy decoding and beam search do, has the opposite problem: once the model repeats a phrase, repeating it again becomes more likely, and beam search loops on "Universidad Nacional Autónoma de México." Human text lies between these extremes.

Decoding tricks such as temperature and top-$p$ sampling, which samples only from the smallest set of tokens covering probability $p$, help by trimming the tail. They cannot, however, make the model prefer answers that are right or useful, because likelihood contains no such preference. Post-training adds it.

### 1.3 Entropy and model confidence

Token-level entropy measures the uncertainty of the model when predicting the next token:

$$
H_t = -\sum_v p_\theta(v \mid x_{<t})\log p_\theta(v \mid x_{<t}).
$$

Low entropy indicates high confidence, while high entropy indicates greater uncertainty. However, a confident prediction is not necessarily correct.

Recall from variational inference that the ELBO contains an entropy term:

$$
\mathrm{ELBO} = \mathbb{E}_{z\sim q}[\log p(x,z\mid\theta)] + H(q).
$$

The entropy term encourages diversity in the variational distribution. In language generation, too much entropy may produce incoherent text, while too little entropy may lead to repetitive responses.

### 1.4 Top-k and top-p sampling

Both top-k and top-p sampling improve generation by removing unlikely tokens during decoding.

- **Top-k sampling:** selects a fixed number of tokens with the highest probabilities.
- **Top-p sampling:** selects the smallest set of tokens whose cumulative probability reaches a threshold $p$.

$$
V^{(p)} = \text{smallest set such that }
\sum_{v\in V^{(p)}}p_\theta(v\mid x_{<t}) \geq p.
$$

The probabilities of the selected tokens are then normalized:

$$
p'(v\mid x_{<t}) =
\frac{p_\theta(v\mid x_{<t})}
{\sum_{u\in V^{(p)}}p_\theta(u\mid x_{<t})},
\quad v\in V^{(p)}.
$$

Unlike top-k, top-p adjusts the number of candidate tokens based on the model's confidence. Both methods preserve randomness while reducing the chance of generating incoherent text.

### 1.5 Improving the training objective

Another approach is to modify the training objective rather than only changing the decoding process.

**Entropy bonus.** An additional entropy term encourages the model to maintain diversity:

$$
\max_\theta \;
\mathbb{E}[\log p_\theta(x)]
+\lambda\sum_t H[p_\theta(\cdot\mid x_{<t})].
$$

**Label smoothing.** Instead of assigning all target probability to the correct token, a small amount of probability is distributed among other tokens:

$$
y_{\text{true}}=1-\epsilon,
\qquad
y_{\text{other}}=\frac{\epsilon}{V-1}.
$$

This helps prevent excessive confidence during training.

**Unlikelihood training.** The model is penalized for assigning high probabilities to undesirable tokens, such as repeated tokens:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{UL}}
=-\sum_{c\in C_t}\log(1-p_\theta(c\mid x_{<t})).
$$

**Scheduled sampling.** During training, the model normally receives the true previous tokens, but during generation it relies on its own predictions. Scheduled sampling reduces this mismatch by occasionally feeding the model its own predictions during training.

These methods address several limitations of maximum likelihood training, but they do not directly optimize the usefulness of model responses. This motivates post-training based on rewards and preferences.


## 2. Post-training optimizes a reward

Post-training treats the model as a *policy* $\pi_\theta$ that answers a prompt $x$ with a response $y$, and it maximizes a reward instead of likelihood:

$$
\max_\theta \; \mathbb{E}_{x,\; y \sim \pi_\theta}\big[R(x, y)\big].
$$

The difficulty is that no one can write down $R$. Every post-training method is therefore defined by **where it gets its reward signal**:

| Method | Source of the reward signal |
|---|---|
| SFT (supervised fine-tuning) | demonstrations of good responses |
| RLHF (RL from human feedback) | a reward model learned from human comparisons |
| DPO (direct preference optimization) | the likelihood of preference pairs |
| RLVR (RL from verifiable rewards) | a program that checks the answer (next lecture) |

## 3. SFT imitates demonstrations

The simplest reward signal is a set of good examples. Supervised fine-tuning, also called **behavior cloning**, trains the model to reproduce them. It teaches instruction following well, but it overfits quickly and cannot express trade-offs between answers.

### 3.1 The SFT loss is the pretraining loss, masked to the response

SFT trains on pairs $(x_i, y_i)$ of prompts and good responses. InstructGPT <d-cite key="ouyang2022training"></d-cite> popularized the approach, with human labelers writing responses to prompts that users had sent to the OpenAI API.
The loss is the same next-token loss used in pretraining, applied only to response tokens:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{SFT}}(\theta) = -\sum_{t=1}^{|y|} \log p_\theta(y_t \mid x, y_{<t}).
$$

In the sequence `<user> What is 2+2 ? <assistant> 2+2 = 4 . <end>`, the prompt tokens serve only as context, and each response token is a target.
During training, each target is predicted from the *true* previous tokens, a scheme called **teacher forcing**. During generation, the model conditions on its *own* previous tokens, so its errors can compound.

The SFT set is tiny, roughly $10^4$–$10^5$ demonstrations against about $10^{13}$ pretraining tokens, but we trust it more, so a separate stage lets us weight it heavily.

### 3.2 Instruction tuning teaches a general skill

FLAN <d-cite key="wei2022finetuned"></d-cite> rewrote more than 60 datasets as natural-language instructions ("Translate to French: ...", "Is this review positive or negative? ...") and fine-tuned one 137B model on all of them.
On task types it never saw in training, that model beat zero-shot 175B GPT-3 on 20 of 25 tasks, and its advantage grew with more tasks and larger models.
FLAN's model had learned the general skill of **following an instruction**, not just the individual tasks.

### 3.3 SFT overfits within a few epochs

A few thousand demonstrations cannot constrain billions of parameters for long. After a few epochs, the model memorizes the demonstrations: its training loss keeps falling, but its validation loss turns upward.
The standard remedy applies here too: watch the validation loss and keep the checkpoint where it is lowest, usually after one to three epochs.

Overfitting is not SFT's only limit. Because SFT imitates text, it copies **surface behavior**, and nothing forces it to learn why a response is good. Because it sees one response per prompt, it also cannot represent **trade-offs between responses** that are almost equally good.

### 3.4 Distillation is SFT on a teacher's outputs

When the demonstrations come from a stronger model instead of from people, SFT becomes **distillation** <d-cite key="hinton2015distilling"></d-cite>. The dataset is $(x_i, y_{\text{teacher}})$, and the loss is unchanged: $-\mathbb{E}_x \log p_\theta(y_{\text{teacher}} \mid x)$.
Distillation transfers abilities such as reasoning into smaller models that are cheaper to run; the Phi models, Gemma, and many open reasoning models are built this way.
Distilling another lab's model is contested; in February 2026, Anthropic reported such distillation of Claude as a terms-of-service violation.

## 4. Preferences capture what demonstrations miss

A good response balances competing goals: accuracy against simplicity, brevity against completeness, caution against usefulness. Often no single answer is best, so a single demonstration cannot capture the trade-off. **Comparisons** can, and a reward model turns them into a score we can optimize.

### 4.1 A comparison says which of two responses is better

A preference example is a triple $(x, y_w, y_l)$ with $y_w \succ y_l$: for prompt $x$, the "winner" $y_w$ is preferred to the "loser" $y_l$.
The symbol $\succ$ denotes a **partial order**, since we can compare two responses to the same prompt but not responses to different prompts.
From such triples, the model learns **relative quality** instead of copying one example.

People find comparisons easier to give than demonstrations: they may not be able to write an ideal answer, but they can usually say which of two answers they prefer. Chat interfaces collect this data by showing two responses side by side or by asking labelers to rank several outputs.

### 4.2 A reward model is a logistic regression on score differences

The Bradley–Terry model <d-cite key="bradley1952rank"></d-cite>, from the 1950s, turns comparisons into scores. It models the probability of a preference as the sigmoid of a reward difference:

$$
P(y_w \succ y_l \mid x) = \sigma\big(r_\phi(x, y_w) - r_\phi(x, y_l)\big),
\qquad
\mathcal{L}_{\text{RM}}(\phi) = -\mathbb{E}\Big[\log \sigma\big(r_\phi(x, y_w) - r_\phi(x, y_l)\big)\Big].
$$

The reward model $r_\phi$ is usually the SFT model with its output layer replaced by a **scalar head** that maps (prompt, response) to a score.
Training it is logistic regression on the score difference.
Because only differences enter the loss, adding a constant to every reward changes nothing: **rewards have no absolute scale**.

### 4.3 The recipe began with a simulated backflip

Christiano et al. <d-cite key="christiano2017deep"></d-cite> introduced this recipe for robotics. A person compared pairs of short video clips of a simulated agent, a reward model was fit to the comparisons, and an RL agent maximized the learned reward, producing new clips to compare.
With about 900 comparisons, under an hour of human time and feedback on under 1% of the agent's interactions, the agent learned a backflip.
The recipe suits any goal that is **easy to recognize but hard to specify**, and InstructGPT applied it to text.

## 5. RLHF optimizes the learned reward, on a leash

RLHF trains the policy to maximize the reward model's score while staying close to the SFT model. It works well, but because the reward model is only a proxy, pushing too hard leads to **reward hacking**, and human preferences do not guarantee **truth**.

  </figcaption>
</figure>

### 5.1 The KL term keeps the policy where the reward model is reliable

RLHF maximizes the expected reward minus a penalty for moving away from a frozen reference model $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, which is the SFT model:

$$
\max_\theta \; \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\; y\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}\big[r_\phi(x, y)\big] \;-\; \beta\, \mathrm{KL}\big(\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x) \,\|\, \pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\big).
$$

The penalty exists because the reward model was trained on responses similar to those of $\pi_{\text{ref}}$; on very different responses, its scores are unreliable.
The weight $\beta$ sets the length of the leash. As $\beta \to 0$, the policy chases the reward model anywhere and exploits its errors; as $\beta \to \infty$, the policy never leaves the SFT model.
The penalty also keeps the policy's outputs fluent and diverse, because the policy cannot collapse onto one high-scoring answer.

### 5.2 The policy learns from its own samples

Each RL step samples a response $y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x)$, scores it with $R(y) = r_\phi(x, y) - \beta \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}$, and raises the probability of $y$ if its score beats the average.
We cannot backpropagate through sampling discrete tokens, so the VAE's reparameterization trick does not apply. Instead, the **log-derivative trick**, $\nabla \pi = \pi \nabla \log \pi$, gives the policy gradient:

$$
\nabla_\theta \sum_y \pi_\theta(y\mid x) R(y) = \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_\theta}\big[R(y)\, \nabla_\theta \log\pi_\theta(y\mid x)\big].
$$

Subtracting a baseline $b$, such as the average reward, from $R(y)$ reduces the variance of this estimate. InstructGPT used PPO <d-cite key="schulman2017proximal"></d-cite>, a stabilized version of the same update.
What distinguishes this from SFT is the training targets: RLHF learns from the **model's own samples**, weighted by reward, not from fixed human responses.

### 5.3 Pushed too far, the policy hacks the reward

Because the reward model only approximates what people want, a policy that optimizes it hard enough finds outputs the reward model overrates. This is Goodhart's law, and its symptom is a reward-model score that keeps rising while human ratings fall <d-cite key="gao2023scaling"></d-cite>.

Three remedies help: a larger $\beta$, earlier stopping, and a better or larger reward model.

### 5.4 RLHF made a small model beat a large one

InstructGPT applied SFT, then a reward model, then PPO. Human raters preferred the outputs of its **1.3B** RLHF model to those of **175B** GPT-3, a model more than 100 times larger.

### 5.5 Human preferences do not guarantee truth

In 2023, John Schulman proposed using RL to reduce hallucinations <d-cite key="schulman2023rlhf"></d-cite>. His reward pays most for a confident correct answer, less for a hedged one, nothing for "I don't know," and penalizes wrong answers, especially confident ones:

| Response | Reward |
|---|---|
| confident and correct | 1 |
| hedged and correct | 0.5 |
| "I don't know" | 0 |
| hedged and wrong | −2 |
| confident and wrong | −4 |

InstructGPT's own evaluations, however, show that RLHF did not reduce hallucinations. Compared with GPT, InstructGPT did improve on TruthfulQA (0.413 vs. 0.224) and on appropriateness. But on hallucinations, where lower is better, SFT alone scored 0.078 and InstructGPT, which adds RLHF, scored 0.172.
The lecturer's explanation: users usually cannot tell when a model is wrong, since if they knew the answer they would not ask. A reward built from their preferences can make answers more convincing without making them more **true**.

## 6. DPO skips the reward model

RLHF needs two models and an unstable RL loop. DPO <d-cite key="rafailov2023direct"></d-cite> shows that the RLHF objective has a closed-form solution, and that solution lets us train the policy directly on preference pairs with an ordinary **classification loss**.

### 6.1 The language model is secretly a reward model

The derivation takes three steps. (You need the idea, not the algebra.)

First, for a fixed reward $r$, the RLHF objective is maximized by

$$
\pi^*(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\, \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\, \exp\!\big(r(x,y)/\beta\big),
$$

where $Z(x)$ normalizes the distribution. This holds because the objective equals $-\beta\,\mathrm{KL}(\pi\,\|\,\pi^*) + \beta\log Z(x)$, and a KL divergence is smallest, zero, when $\pi = \pi^*$.

Second, solving for $r$ shows that a reward is a log-ratio of policies:

$$
r(x, y) = \beta \log\frac{\pi^*(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} + \beta\log Z(x).
$$

Third, Bradley–Terry uses only the difference $r(x, y_w) - r(x, y_l)$, so the hard-to-compute $\beta\log Z(x)$ cancels.
What remains is an implicit reward defined by the policy itself:

$$
\hat r_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}.
$$

### 6.2 The DPO loss is Bradley–Terry on the implicit reward

Substituting $\hat r_\theta$ into the reward-model loss gives

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)}\left[\log\sigma\!\left(\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w\mid x)} - \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right].
$$

This loss raises the chosen response and lowers the rejected one, both measured relative to $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, and it puts the most weight on pairs the model currently ranks the wrong way.
Its $\beta$ plays the same role as in RLHF: a larger $\beta$ keeps $\pi_\theta$ closer to $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.
At initialization, $\pi_\theta = \pi_{\text{ref}}$, both log-ratios are zero, and the loss equals $-\log\sigma(0) = \log 2 \approx 0.693$, a useful sanity check.

### 6.3 The loss tracks the margin, not either response

Because the loss depends only on the **margin** between the two log-ratios, both log-ratios can fall while the loss improves. In that case, probability moves away from both responses toward others, but the rejected response loses probability faster.

### 6.4 RLHF can explore; DPO is simpler

| | RLHF (e.g., PPO) | DPO |
|---|---|---|
| Reward | explicit reward model $r_\phi$ | implicit: $\beta\log(\pi_\theta/\pi_{\text{ref}})$ |
| Training data | fresh samples from the policy | a fixed set of preference pairs |
| Optimization | RL loop with policy gradients | one supervised-style loss |
| Strength | can improve beyond the given pairs | simple, stable, cheap |
| Weakness | unstable, expensive, reward hacking | limited to its pairs; can overfit them |

Neither method gives us everything. The ideal signal would let us generate unlimited new data, as RLHF does, and would say exactly whether each answer is right. That signal is a verifiable reward, the topic of the next lecture.

## 7. Post-training in practice

In practice, post-training data comes from two sources. **Models** supply it through distillation or synthetic generation, as in Self-Instruct <d-cite key="wang2023selfinstruct"></d-cite> and UltraChat <d-cite key="ding2023enhancing"></d-cite>, where a teacher generates tasks and responses and a judge filters them. **People**, especially a model's own users, supply demonstrations and, more often, preferences.

Whatever its source, the reward signal brings five common failures. **Reward hacking**: the policy learns the reward model's artifacts. **Preference overfitting**: human preferences are not truth. **Mode collapse**: RLHF reduces response diversity. **Distribution shift**: alignment trained on narrow prompts may not transfer. **Over-confidence**: people prefer confident answers, so RLHF rewards confidence even when the model is uncertain.

Modern LLMs are therefore a **layered pipeline**, not a single algorithm: pretraining learns the distribution of language, SFT imitates desired behavior, preference learning learns a reward, RL or DPO optimizes for that reward, and PEFT methods such as LoRA adapt the model efficiently. Each stage after pretraining adds what likelihood lacks, so that a fluent model becomes a useful one.
